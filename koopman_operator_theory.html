From Chaos to Order

Imagine flying a drone on a calm afternoon. A sudden gust of wind hits. You push the joystick right to compensate, but the drone violently pitches forward, oscillating wildly until it crashes. That frustration is your direct encounter with a fundamental truth of the universe: Non-linearity.

In a perfect, linear world, pushing an object with 1 unit of force moves it 1 unit. But reality is a chaotic web of variables. To survive, engineers built workarounds like the PID (Proportional-Integral-Derivative) Controller.

// THE PID COMPROMISE

The PID algorithm ignores the complexity of the wind and focuses purely on the "error" between the target state and the actual state:

$$ u(t) = K_p e(t) + K_i \int_{0}^{t} e(\tau) d\tau + K_d \frac{de(t)}{dt} $$

It linearizes the problem locally, second by second. But what if we didn't have to compromise? What if we could parse the entire chaotic storm as a globally linear equation from the start?

The Man Who Saw The Future

In 1931, while physics was split between classical mechanics (predicting planets, wildly non-linear) and quantum mechanics (predicting atoms, perfectly linear), an American mathematician named Bernard Koopman asked a dangerous question: "Can we borrow the perfect, linear math of the quantum realm and apply it to the chaotic classical world?"

Usually, we track a system by its state $x$. This yields equations like $x_{t+1} = f(x_t)$, where $f$ is a chaotic nightmare. Koopman suggested we stop looking at the state directly and instead look through the lens of Observables—functions of the state, denoted as $g(x)$.

He introduced the Koopman Operator ($K$), proving a mind-bending mathematical exploit:

$$ K g(x_t) = g(x_{t+1}) = g(f(x_t)) $$

The operator $K$ is always linear. He found the exact spell to render absolute chaos completely linear.

The Rule of Shifting Perspectives

Think of a factory floor with thousands of moving gears. Tracking every gear (the state) is impossible. Instead, you sit in a control room and monitor "gauges" (temperature, power output). These gauges are Koopman's Observables.

Instead of asking "Where will this gear move?", Koopman asks "How will the value on this gauge change over time?" By doing this, the relationship between today's gauge reading and tomorrow's gauge reading becomes a perfectly straight line.

But every magic trick has a cost. To make this work flawlessly, you can't just use one or two gauges. You might need an infinite number of them.

DMD: Unlocking Data Secrets

In 2010, the bridge between Koopman's abstract math and our hard drives was built: Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD).

DMD ingests snapshots of a system over time, organizing them into two matrices: $X$ (current state) and $Y$ (next state). The algorithm's goal is to find the matrix $A$ that maps $X$ to $Y$:

$$ Y \approx A X \quad \implies \quad A = Y X^+ $$

By computing the pseudoinverse ($X^+$), the computer extracts the spatial structures, frequencies, and growth rates of the chaos. It is entirely equation-free. It doesn't care about the laws of physics; it just extracts the linear truth from the noise.
